SANKALP

Automatic Block Planning · SIH 2026 PS 26027 · Ministry of Railways · Team CodeCraft
Prototype on a SIMULATED division · not Indian Railways field data

How to read this page. A block (or line possession) is a scheduled period when a stretch of track is handed to a maintenance department and no trains may run on it. Three departments — Engineering, Signal & Telecom, and Traction Distribution — each request them separately today, so the same section gets shut more than once in a week.

The two charts below show the same backlog (— defects) planned two ways. Rows are track sections, the horizontal axis is one week of hours. Grey shading behind the bars is train traffic — darker means busier, so the pale bands are the night maintenance corridors.

The numbers are real output from the solver, not illustrations — but the division, timetable, crew limits, defects and due dates are simulated, and “rule-based baseline” is our own model of current practice (each department gets two fixed block days a week), not Indian Railways data. Every comparison here is against that baseline, scored identically.

Efficiency — the honest comparison

Raw cost is not comparable between two plans that deliver different amounts of maintenance: a plan that simply defers everything trivially scores zero possessions and zero paths lost. So we divide cost by the maintenance actually delivered. These are the numbers to judge us on.

Rule-based baseline — our model of current practice

Each department plans inside its own allotted block days, with no coordination between them. Look for the same row being used on two different days — that is one section shut twice where once would have done. It uses the same urgency order, night corridors and crew limits as SANKALP; only cross-department coordination is missing.

SANKALP-Net — survival model

For every defect it predicts a hazard curve: the probability of failure on each of the coming days. The solver reads the whole curve, so a fast-deteriorating defect is pulled earlier — risk decides when work happens, not only whether. The network never schedules anything; it supplies evidence, and the constraint solver decides.

Solver

Google OR-Tools CP-SAT in two stages. Stage 1 solves the due dates first: the fewest due jobs undone, then the fewest days late — and proves that minimum, using no AI at all. Stage 2 optimises risk, trains and possessions only among plans that stay on that floor, so efficiency can never be bought with a missed deadline. Stage 2 is time-boxed and reports FEASIBLE, not OPTIMAL: a better plan may exist, and we do not claim otherwise. (Due dates here are set by the simulator; real IRPWM / SEM / ACTM codal dates load unchanged.)

Why this model — benchmarked, not asserted

Every model below is trained on the same records with the same temporal split and scored the same way. C-index measures ranking; IBS measures the accuracy of the whole survival curve over time, which is what the scheduler actually consumes. LightGBM, the original approach, is the best 7-day yes/no classifier in this table (AUC 0.810) — but it produces one number per defect and no curve at all, so it structurally cannot tell the scheduler when risk rises. (It could be retrained as a hazard model; we did not test that, and the “single score” arm in our A/B is our own curve collapsed to one number, not LightGBM's output.)

Reported honestly: removing the graph encoder, or collapsing three department heads to one, leaves prediction statistically unchanged across five random seeds. That is expected — failure probability is driven by load and weather, while network position drives consequence. So topology is used where it genuinely earns its place: in the route-aware scheduler, where it is the reason trains are rerouted rather than cancelled.
